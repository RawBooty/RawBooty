AI is Fundamentally Changing the Internets Revenue model?

@rawbooty2 on X.com

Version 1.0 · November 11, 2026

Artificial Intelligence is destroying the revenue model the internet was literally built on.

Right now, at this very moment we’re at what is generally, and correctly IMO, being viewed as a major fulcrum point in the history of internet. Only, not, for the reasons most have yet recognized.

In my opinion, where investors need to be focused is on how AI is converting the very nature of the internet from a “discovery economy” into an “outcome economy.”

What does this mean?

Historically, if you needed something as simple as a new toothbrush, you'd open Google or Bing and begin a research process. You'd type in a few keywords, visit several websites, read reviews, compare dozens of competing products, and eventually make a purchase.

Later, as Amazon and Google became the dominant gateways to commerce and discovery, the process became more centralized but no less frustrating. You'd go to Amazon, scroll through dozens of toothbrushes, read dozens of reviews, become exhausted by the choices, and eventually buy the Xiang-Foo UltraCavityPlus9000, whose bristles fell out in your mouth after a week of use.

AI is changing behavior, yet the problem is the same. You still need a new toothbrush, only instead of going to amazon or google. You’re going to an AI and asking:

“who makes the best toothbrush, last time I bought a Xiang-Foo UltraCavityPlus9000 from Amazon and the bristles fell out in my mouth. I want something better, who makes the best toothbrush?”

and your agent is dutifully responding, with confidence:

"If you want an electric toothbrush, the Philips Sonicare 9900 Prestige. If you prefer a manual toothbrush, many dental professionals and enthusiasts regard the Curaprox CS 5460 as one of the best available."

If the answer isn’t to your liking, you might follow up with:

"What's a cheaper alternative?" AI responds: "The Philips Sonicare ProtectiveClean 6100."

Point being: you never read a review, you never went to a website and scrolled through products, and you never bought a Chinese knockoff that fell apart because you were too lazy, busy, or embarrassed to return it.

You had a question.

You asked a question.

You got an answer.

This is a very big deal.

What's happening right now is that consumers, across all income levels and walks of life, are being retrained to expect good solutions without the traditional burden of research, experimentation, or, perhaps most importantly, advertising consumption.

Again, this is a very big deal.

Now, we need to be clear: this is not an all-or-nothing outcome. Not everyone is going to abandon traditional methods, and not every problem will be solved by an AI agent, or by an AI agent alone.

But that ultimately doesn't matter.

For companies that built their business models around historical patterns of consumer behavior, even a marginal change in those behaviors can become a major problem.

Consider a 10-20% reduction in traffic. To many people, that may not sound significant. To the average internet business, it can be existential.

Many of these companies were built around enormous fixed-cost structures, highly optimized conversion funnels, and assumptions about user engagement that have remained largely unchanged for decades.

Even modest declines in user traffic, ad impressions, affiliate clicks, or transaction volume can have an outsized impact on profitability because the underlying infrastructure was built for a scale that may no longer exist.

Last week, in late October 2026, Reddit shocked many investors during its earnings call when CEO Steve Huffman admitted:

"AI mode does not drive traffic. Not for us and not to anybody as far as I know. It used to be a traffic driver. Now it's not."

The stock market correctly freaked out and sold the stock off roughly 30% that day.

But here’s the thing people missed. What Huffman is seeing isn't actually traffic problem. It's a fundamental shift in the utility of the network itself.

What he either doesn't realize, or doesn't want to admit to investors, is that Reddit's utility was largely derived from Google's failure. As search became increasingly commercialized, users discovered that appending the word "reddit" to a query often produced better results than trusting Google alone.

Search engines failed to provide a useful answer, so in response users often tried Reddit next.

But for most people, Reddit was rarely the primary destination.

It was usually “Plan B.”

Sadly, for Huffman, “Plan B” was just demoted to “Plan C.”

Yesterday, Plan B was Reddit.

Today, “Plan B” is Google or Bing.

The new “Plan A” is an AI agent.

What's happening is not the destruction of traffic, but a reordering of traffic.

This means, that if your business model depends on serving advertising impressions in exchange for low-cost access to human attention? Then you have a very serious problem.

Now here’s where things get really interesting, and tricky.

Companies like Alphabet / Google, Meta and others are looking at this new tech and thinking, “I need to own this platform. If people use my agent, I can eventually find ways to serve people the ads I’ve been serving them via the new platform. This is a growth opportunity.”

This is, in my opinion, a fantasy.

AI is fundamentally a probabilistic engine. It does not reason the way humans do. It evaluates vast numbers of relationships and returns the solution it believes have the highest probability of satisfying the request. While these systems can certainly be influenced, tuned, or commercialized, their utility depends on providing useful answers.

Historically Google has been able to force me to scroll past ten advertisements. Meta was able to force me to watch three sponsored reels, but, an AI that consistently recommends the Xiang-Foo UltraCavityPlus9000 over demonstrably superior alternatives? This model eventually loses user trust, and trust is the entire product.

Think about it like this. Historically, if a typical user wanted a chainsaw their process would look something like:

  1. Search Google “chainsaw”

  2. Read reviews on chainsaws.

  3. Watch YouTube videos about chainsaws

  4. Browse Home Depot for pricing and product details

  5. See ads on chainsaws

  6. Buy a mid-range chainsaw as a result of the above process

Importantly, at every step in this process advertising opportunities were generated.

Now, think about how it works today. I simply ask the agent, "What's the best homeowner chainsaw for around $400?"

Research project: Done.

Further, notice that it doesn't really matter which major AI you ask. The wording may differ, the ranking may differ, but the answers tend to converge because they are all attempting to solve the same problem using the same underlying data. A good chainsaw remains a good chainsaw regardless of whether you're asking OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, xAI, or Microsoft.

Now, Micha Kaufman, CEO of Fiverr, said something interesting about agent loyalty on the q3 conf call. He said, simple that ‘Humans have loyalty.” This is an incredibly profound statement, and an easy one to overlook.

Think about it, people naturally have favorite stores, favorite brands, favorite websites, and favorite platforms.

AI agents have no loyalty, only probabilities. An agent does not care whether the answer comes from Amazon, Walmart, Home Depot, a local dealer, or even the crackhead on the corner. All it cares about is finding the answer most likely to be correct.

That's a terrifying thought if your moat is primarily distribution.

For twenty-five years, I've watched a handful of companies grow into trillion-dollar enterprises by capturing and monetizing distribution.

Search was distribution. ($GOOG)

Social media was distribution. ($META)

E-commerce was distribution.($AMZN)

Advertising was distribution.

AI does not care who owns the distribution channel. It cares about producing the best outcome.

Now, if you're a company that makes a product or provides a service, there's another problem entirely:

How do you advertise in this new world?

If you're a category leader like Stihl, the wind is suddenly at your back. AI is likely to direct more customers toward you because your reputation, quality, and performance have already been established, at no additional cost to you.

If you're a middle-tier brand like our fictional Xiang-Foo, life just became far more difficult.

Historically, Xiang-Foo could buy ads, game search rankings, manipulate reviews, flood marketplaces with inventory, and rely on buyer fatigue to win sales.

In an outcome economy, none of those advantages matter nearly as much.

The only thing that matters is whether your product is actually good enough to be recommended.

For decades, sales was largely a game of persuasion. Trust was low. Information was scarce. Customers were poorly informed and often lacked access to experts.

Today, every customer effectively has a digital expert working on their behalf, for free.

That's the central problem.

The winners and losers of the next decade will be determined by who understands that distinction first.

Now, if you think this has no implications for AWS, are you sure?

Ask yourself a simple question:

How many AWS customers chose Amazon because they lacked the tools, knowledge, time, or confidence to properly evaluate alternative providers?

How many selected AWS simply because nobody ever got fired for choosing Amazon?

What happens when every customer suddenly has access to an AI capable of comparing providers, architectures, pricing models, performance characteristics, and operational tradeoffs in seconds?

What if a lesser-known competitor offers a better solution for that specific customer?

Anyhow, just some food for thought.
