Social Diversity vs Biological Reality: Why Forced Integration Keeps Failing

Policy decisions of recent decades are now coming to a visible head, and one way or another, society must face reality, no matter how uncomfortable or ugly the truth may be.

The best way I know to approach this honestly is to first examine how the same forces play out in the rest of the natural world. So, let’s shift the argument away from humans for a moment and look to the animal kingdom where divergence in populations happens constantly, for all sorts of reasons.

To start, let’s imagine a flock of fifty birds. All of them are plain grey, living in easy social harmony. Then one day a gene flips, and a single bird is born with one bright yellow feather on its head. In every other way it’s identical to the rest. As the lone odd bird, it’s treated a bit differently — an anomaly — but it’s still mostly accepted by the flock as non-threatening.

Now fast-forward a few generations. That yellow feather trait has been passed on to new generations. Where once there was a single bird, there are now ten or twenty birds with it. Optics suddenly change. The yellow-feathered birds and the plain grey ones begin naturally clustering into distinct groups. Interbreeding still occurs, especially when mates are scarce, but a clear bias toward segregation emerges.

This is natural genetic divergence in action.

This isn’t just a hypothetical or my personal observation; it’s a well-documented tendency seen across virtually every species where researchers have looked. One of the cleanest and most thoroughly studied examples is the Gouldian finch. These birds are genetically and physically almost identical to one another except for one major visible trait: head color (red, black, or the rare yellow). In both wild and captive populations, they reliably show strong preferences for associating and mating with birds of their own head color. As the different morphs increase in number, they form distinct subgroups even while living in the same area.

This pattern extends beyond simple appearance into behavior as well. One striking demonstration comes from research on aggression in spiders. When scientists selectively breed for high aggression, the trait quickly becomes so pronounced that it overrides even the most basic drive to reproduce. The most aggressive individuals often kill potential mates or rivals before breeding can occur, making the population unsustainable. In essence, the genetic push toward aggression becomes self-defeating.

Similar dynamics has also been observed in eusocial insects like bees and ants. In these instances, colony-level aggression traits, such as how readily they sting or recruit nestmates for defense, are also strongly heritable and can be rapidly amplified or reduced over just a few generations.

The takeaway is clear: aggression is not purely cultural or learned behavior. It is a genetically influenced trait shaped by evolution. In humans, psychopathy represents one of the more extreme expressions of this same underlying machinery. We can reasonably posit that other behavioral tendencies are also genetically biased.

Now, let’s be honest, it’s easy to dismiss spiders, birds, and bees as “just animals.” We like to think of humans as uniquely special; and perhaps we are in many ways. But these examples still provide crucial context for what’s playing out in human societies today. Massive bodies of research from biology, psychology, anthropology, and genetics all reinforce the same pattern: these deep biases toward grouping with our those most similar to ourselves are real and ancient. The simplest explanation is that this is partly how evolution works. It’s likely a fundamental organizing principle that has driven speciation for millions of years.

This conclusion, however, reveals a deeply uncomfortable truth; both socially and personally. It becomes especially stark when you confront the actual, measurable genetic variety among humans, which is truly staggering.

Modern genetics has confirmed something remarkable: Africa alone contains more human genetic diversity than the rest of the world combined. Think about that for a moment: two random Africans are often more genetically different from each other than a European is from an East Asian. This is not what most people expect, but it makes perfect sense once you understand that non-African genetic diversity is largely a small subset of African diversity. This is the result of a relatively small group of early humans migrating out of Africa and going through a significant genetic bottleneck.

It’s also worth noting that the very term “Homo sapiens” is something of a misnomer. All modern humans are genetic mixtures of multiple archaic human lineages. DNA from Neanderthals, Denisovans, and other pre-modern populations (possibly including Homo erectus and related groups) has been mixed into our genome. Further, this mixing is not uniform. It varies significantly across populations. Most notably, Neanderthal ancestry is present in nearly all non-African populations (typically 1–2%) but is absent or extremely low in sub-Saharan Africans. Other archaic contributions show the reverse pattern in some African groups.

It is for these reasons that I find the narrow, almost obsessive focus on “Black” populations particularly distasteful. In truth, I’ve never liked the terms “Black,” “White,” or even “Asian.” These are crude, arbitrary bins that gloss over a much deeper reality: every human being is a complex mix of both cultural and genetic factors.

If we’re going to discuss this honestly as a society, we need far more precision than three crude racial baskets can possibly provide. The problem is that genuine precision gets messy very quickly, as we’ve seen with the recent, ever-expanding efforts to re-classify gender and identity through the proliferating LGBTQIA+ spectrum and its endless variants.

If we were operating purely as biologists, we might naturally look toward models like speciation or subspecies. That framework has been correctly rejected. Interestingly, it wasn’t rejected because it violates the genetic data, but because it fails to account for the enormous influence of culture, shared values, language, religion, and social norms that shape how human groups actually form and function.

That said, these broad racial categories are extremely useful politically. They function as highly effective propaganda tools that reinforce red-team/blue-team tribalism which in turn keeps the political class empowered and the public divided on the politicians entirely unnatural terms. And this, in my view, is the true root of many of our problems today.

Which brings us directly to our political systems. What most people find surprising is that in a pure democracy, where every person gets an equal vote regardless of traits, abilities, beliefs, or competence, politicians are strongly incentivized to ignore or actively flatten biological and cultural realities. What often dominates is not the numerical majority, but an organized, motivated coalition that may actually be a statistical minority.

By its very structure, pure democracy encourages unnatural alliances, suppresses honest discussion of differences, and rewards division. This is classic divide-and-conquer dynamics at work, which tends to build long-term resentment and conflict rather than resolve it.

It is for this reason that the United States was deliberately designed as a republic, not a pure democracy. Having learned hard lessons from antiquity, the Founders intentionally created safeguards against direct majority rule. Under the original system, senators were not elected directly by the people. Instead, they were chosen by state legislatures. This kept senators accountable to their state governments rather than national popular opinion, encouraging them to focus on qualified representation and long-term interests instead of pandering to the passions of the national electorate.

Where America diverged was with the passage of the 17th Amendment in 1913, which established the direct election of senators. This single change corrupted the original constitutional balance. It shifted power away from the states and toward national popularity contests, encouraging senators to pander to mass opinion rather than represent state interests and long-term stability.

This shift was greatly accelerated beginning in 1960 with John F. Kennedy’s campaign push for immigration reform; a movement that culminated in the 1965 Immigration and Nationality Act (Hart-Celler Act). That legislation dismantled the national-origins quota system that had long favored European immigrants who were culturally and genetically more similar to the existing American population. In its place came chain migration and explicit “diversity” priorities that dramatically altered the country’s demographic trajectory.

Now, let’s be very clear: we do have countless examples of people working and living together successfully across physical and cultural differences. When there is a strong common purpose, whether in the military, elite sports teams, tight-knit companies, or small communities facing external pressure cooperation can be genuine, productive, and even inspiring. History is full of such examples.

These pockets of successful cooperation create a powerful illusion. They make us believe that stable, long-term, large-scale co-habitation across deep differences is not only possible, but inevitable; and therefore, the optimal way to organize society.

This, however, conceals important nuance. Small dissimilar groups can and often do thrive inside a larger foreign population. The reason is deeply human: people have a powerful need for social peers — others who understand them, reflect them, and make them feel “normal.”

Interestingly, non-conforming individuals almost always seek each other out. Even when they come from very different backgrounds, shared outsider status or a common cause can create surprisingly strong bonds. You see this pattern everywhere; at work, in school cafeterias, in neighborhoods, churches, and social circles across the world. High school cliques are the perfect everyday example: even in largely homogeneous schools, kids naturally sort into jocks, nerds, theater kids, stoners, artists, etc. The drive to feel understood and accepted is that strong.

The real problems only surface when conflict incentives emerge. These arise whenever one group perceives another as a threat — whether existential (to safety, resources, identity, or continued existence) or as an opportunity for predation and, or, status-seeking. Weaker or visibly distinct groups are frequently targeted not just for survival reasons, but as a way for stronger groups to demonstrate dominance, gain prestige, or extract resources.

Once this dynamic appears, the deep genetic machinery of “us versus them” activates with brutal reliability. Visible physical differences like skin color, facial features, body type often serve as the fastest, most reliable markers for this tribal response. Cultural differences (diet, religion, values, customs) can trigger it just as powerfully. The underlying pattern is the same across all human populations and all of recorded history: when real or perceived competition, threat, or status-seeking opportunities arise, the evolutionary predisposition for tribalism is powerfully reactivated.

Let’s look back to the land of maximum human genetic diversity: Africa. Until roughly 200 years ago, the continent was populated by thousands of small, geographically isolated tribes. For most of their history, these tribes existed in near-constant violent conflict with one another. As a result, groups tended to self-isolate, separated by geographic buffers and deep suspicion of outsiders. Over long periods of time, this isolation cultivated some truly remarkable physiological diversity among them.

Some striking examples include:

  • The Vadoma (“ostrich people”) of Zimbabwe carry a genetic mutation giving many members only two toes per foot.

  • San/Khoisan groups show extreme steatopygia (prominent fat deposits in the buttocks/hips), lighter skin tones relative to other sub-Saharan populations, and unique click languages.

  • Mbuti and Baka Pygmies of the Congo Basin are genetically small-statured, often under 5 ft as adults.

  • Dinka and Nuer (Nilotic peoples) include some of the tallest humans on Earth, with averages over 6 ft and many individuals reaching 7 ft+.

  • The Sherpas of the Himalayas possess distinct genetic adaptations (notably EPAS1 variants) that allow them to thrive at extreme altitudes.

These aren’t random curiosities or mere trivia. They are extreme but real examples of how quickly small, reproductively isolated populations can diverge, both physiologically and behaviorally, when left to their own evolutionary paths.

If we shift our focus to Europeans, populations that were largely similar physiologically, we see the same pattern playing out dramatically. For centuries they slaughtered one another over ethnic, religious, and national lines: the Wars of Religion, the Thirty Years’ War, endless conflicts in the Balkans, Ireland, and beyond. Physiological similarity did not prevent tribalism; it simply meant the flashpoints shifted to language, religion, culture, and perceived national identity.

In more recent history, the neighborhoods of early 20th-century Chicago and New York were sharply fractured by Irish, Italian, Polish, Jewish, and other ethnic enclaves. These divisions were fiercely defended, often with significant gang violence and social hostility.

The point is this: remove one visible flashpoint, and the tribal instinct simply finds the next salient difference. The underlying machinery doesn’t just disappear, it redirects itself. This is the nature of competitive systems.

Which bring us to the real tension: evolution strongly favors survivorship of the capable. Pure, unfiltered competition (in nature or raw capitalism) would sort the lower end of the distribution through harsh outcomes.

Modern society has instead chosen widespread empathy and subsidization of the weak, often at the direct expense of the strong. We do this because it feels humane and compassionate. More importantly, these emotions are incredibly easy to exploit, and they serve powerful political incentives.

The harsh truth is that real progress is almost always driven by capable minorities. The hard work, creativity, and innovation of a relatively small number of people create opportunity and wealth that lift the whole of society. Unfortunately, this naturally produces inequality. As a result, majoritarian political systems are strongly incentivized to redistribute from the productive to the less capable.

This is precisely why pure democracy is so seductive to so many: if you cannot succeed through your own merit, you can always team up with enough others and overwhelm the capable through sheer force of numbers.

The Founders understood this danger clearly. That is precisely why they designed a republic with multiple checks against pure majority rule. What has changed is that our very success in Europe and America has fueled a gradual but relentless progressive shift toward more direct democracy. This shift has intensified the mismatch between groups rather than resolving it. The result is now rapidly cultivating widespread anger, the kind that history shows will eventually manifest in violence if left unaddressed.

Democratic and socialist approaches are, at their very root, unnatural. They work against evolutionary selection pressures and tend to amplify rather than reduce group differences over generations. That’s the uncomfortable core we keep avoiding. It’s also a problem we must begin to accept and face head on. We know what happens if we don’t; the question is do we have the will and the humility to admit our own fallibility?

What we must accept is that human beings did not evolve to ignore differences; we evolved to organize around them — just like every other organism on Earth.

Which presents a problem. The problem is quite easily stated, yet brutally difficult to accept: any realistic path forward requires balancing genuine compassion with unflinching honesty about human nature, incentives, and genetics. Realistically, there is no final peaceful equilibrium waiting for us. Humans did not evolve for perpetual harmony. We evolved to organize around differences, to compete, to form coalitions, and, when incentives align, to dominate others.

With that in mind, we must also accept that globally, diversity is a strength for the species. It provides the raw genetic material for adaptation, resilience, and long-term survival.

But at the scale of lived communities? Where real human beings actually have to coexist day after day; that same diversity often becomes a major source of friction precisely because human nature is what it is. To preserve broad genetic diversity at the species level, we must allow localized homogeneity. We should enable and support people’s natural tendency to cluster by the traits that matter most to them; whether appearance, culture, values, behavior, religion, or any combination of these.

We must do this if we want to reduce violent conflict. Where groups are forced together under conditions of real or perceived competition, the ancient tribal machinery reactivates with grim predictability. We are watching the consequences unfold on a near-daily basis across Europe.

This is not a call for identity politics or state-enforced separation. It is a call to stop fighting human nature and start designing systems that channel it more productively. Stronger shared culture where possible. Rigorous merit-based systems. Voluntary association, including supporting self-segregation where it demonstrably reduces conflict. What we need are far fewer artificial incentives that subsidize failure and punish success.

Given the chance, absent constant political and social engineering, people will self-sort into relatively stable equilibria; just as they always have.

Where governments should intervene is when those groups turn predatory or violent toward the broader order. That said, we should harbor no illusions. The strong will still test the weak. Coalitions will still form. Dominance behaviors will still emerge. The best we can hope for is to build arrangements in which that eternal struggle does less damage to the civilizational fabric; in the aggregate.

The bottom line is this: there is no ideal solution — only less-bad trade-offs. The question we need to be asking is whether we have the humility and courage to choose them consciously, or whether we will keep pretending that human nature can be lectured, shamed, or regulated out of existence.

We need to work with human nature instead of waging war against it.

Final note: This entire discussion still glosses over what technology is doing to human mobility and social organization. Physical isolation is becoming simultaneously more difficult and easier than ever before. The future may not resemble past solutions at all. Never before in human history have social groups been able to form and maintain themselves digitally across vast distances. That said, being friends with someone virtually is very different from living next door to them — especially if your neighbor blasts death metal at 2 a.m., has twelve barking dogs, or dumps their trash in your backyard.
